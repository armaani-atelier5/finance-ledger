Fed Hikes Rates, Markets Bounce Back — Here's What It Means for You

The Federal Reserve raised interest rates for the first time since 2023, rattling Wall Street last week — but markets are recovering today as oil falls and a US-China summit approaches.

Last Wednesday, September 16, the Federal Open Market Committee voted unanimously — all 12 members — to raise the federal funds rate by 25 basis points, lifting it from 3.50–3.75% to 3.75–4.00%. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh confirmed this is the start of a new tightening cycle, not a one-off move, sending a clear message: the inflation fight is not over.

The Fed's new "dot plot" — a chart showing where policymakers expect rates to go — now puts the median forecast at 4.125% by year end, meaning at least one more hike is likely before December 2026. As many as 16 out of 19 FOMC members projected at least one additional hike this year.

What's driving this? Inflation is still sitting at 3.4% year-over-year, well above the Fed's 2% target. Core PCE — the Fed's preferred measure — is even higher at 3.7%. Spiking energy prices from the ongoing Middle East conflict have kept inflation stubborn despite earlier signs of cooling.

Today, markets are recovering strongly. The S&P 500 surged +1.6% to 7,767 and the Nasdaq jumped +2.3% to 27,139 as oil prices fell for a fourth consecutive session, easing inflation fears. Tech and chip stocks are leading the rally, with massive gains in META, INTC, AMD, and ARM ahead of META's developers forum this week.

US-China Trade
Trump-Xi Summit September 24: What's at Stake for Global Markets
President Trump will host Xi Jinping at the White House this Wednesday in the first Chinese state visit in over a decade. The October 2025 trade truce — which swapped US tariff pauses for continued Chinese rare earth exports — expires in roughly five weeks. A $30 billion tariff reduction framework covering goods on both sides is being negotiated. Treasury Secretary Bessent has been leading talks with Chinese Vice Premier He Lifeng. The outcome will directly affect tech supply chains, inflation, and US-China equity markets.

Economy
Bank of Japan Raises Rates to 1.25% — Highest Since 1995
The Bank of Japan raised its policy rate to 1.25% last week, marking its highest level since 1995. In a surprising reaction, the yen fell rather than rose after the announcement, while Japanese government bonds dropped 2.5% in dollar terms, making them the weakest part of the global bond market. This matters for students because Japan's interest rate moves affect global capital flows and currency markets that show up in every finance career.

What This Means for Finance Students
Rate Hike Impact on You
Higher fed funds rate = more expensive borrowing. Credit card interest rates go up. Student loan refinancing gets pricier. But savings account and CD yields also rise — if you have money sitting in a savings account, now is a good time to shop for a high-yield account. This is exactly what the Fed wants: slow down spending, reduce inflation.
Career Implications
Investment banking deal flow slows when rates are high — confirmed by weak Q3 fee guidance from Goldman Sachs and Bank of America. But fixed income desks, rates trading, and credit analysis roles become more active. If you're recruiting, highlight your understanding of interest rate environments. It's the hottest topic in every finance interview right now.

September 2026 — Rates are Rising
"Start tracking your net worth right now — even if the number is small or negative. Assets minus liabilities, updated once a month in a spreadsheet. With the Fed raising rates, high-yield savings accounts are finally paying real returns — move your idle cash there.
