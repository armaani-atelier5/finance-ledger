Fed Hikes Rates, Markets Bounce Back — Here's What It Means for You

The Federal Reserve raised interest rates for the first time since 2023, rattling Wall Street last week — but markets are recovering today as oil falls and a US-China summit approaches.

Last Wednesday, September 16, the Federal Open Market Committee voted unanimously — all 12 members — to raise the federal funds rate by 25 basis points, lifting it from 3.50–3.75% to 3.75–4.00%. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh confirmed this is the start of a new tightening cycle, not a one-off move, sending a clear message: the inflation fight is not over.

The Fed's new "dot plot" — a chart showing where policymakers expect rates to go — now puts the median forecast at 4.125% by year end, meaning at least one more hike is likely before December 2026. As many as 16 out of 19 FOMC members projected at least one additional hike this year.

What's driving this? Inflation is still sitting at 3.4% year-over-year, well above the Fed's 2% target. Core PCE — the Fed's preferred measure — is even higher at 3.7%. Spiking energy prices from the ongoing Middle East conflict have kept inflation stubborn despite earlier signs of cooling.

Today, markets are recovering strongly. The S&P 500 surged +1.6% to 7,767 and the Nasdaq jumped +2.3% to 27,139 as oil prices fell for a fourth consecutive session, easing inflation fears. Tech and chip stocks are leading the rally, with massive gains in META, INTC, AMD, and ARM ahead of META's developers forum this week.

Fed Hikes 25bps — First Increase Since July 2023

In a unanimous vote, the Federal Reserve raised rates and made clear this is the start of a new tightening cycle, not a one-off move.

By Aman Lathiya, Secretary  ·  Finance Club NYC  ·  September 21, 2026

On Wednesday, September 16, the Federal Open Market Committee voted 12–0 to raise the federal funds rate by 25 basis points, from 3.50–3.75% to 3.75–4.00%. It's the Fed's first rate increase since July 2023.

Fed Chair Kevin Warsh described the move as the beginning of a new rate cycle rather than a single adjustment. In plain terms: the Fed is telling markets to expect more.

What the dot plot says

The "dot plot" is a chart where each Fed policymaker marks where they think rates should be at the end of the year. The new median sits at 4.125% for year-end 2026 — meaning at least one more 25bps hike is likely before December. As many as 16 of the 19 FOMC participants projected at least one more increase this year. Markets are currently pricing roughly an 88% chance of another hike in Q4.

Why the Fed is raising rates

Inflation is still running at 3.4% year-over-year, well above the Fed's 2% target. Core PCE, the Fed's preferred gauge, is even higher at 3.7%. Energy prices driven by the Middle East conflict have kept inflation sticky, even after earlier signs it was cooling.

How markets reacted

Stocks sold off after the announcement, and the 10-year Treasury yield touched 5% last week. The dollar strengthened, with the DXY index gaining 0.9% on the week, and gold fell — which is typical when real rates rise, since gold pays no interest. By Monday, markets were recovering as oil prices dropped and the 10-year eased to around 4.98%.

What this means for you

Borrowing gets more expensive: credit card rates, auto loans, and student loan refinancing all move higher. The upside is that savings accounts and CDs pay more too, so if you have cash sitting idle, it's a good time to compare high-yield savings accounts. On the career side, rate environments are the hottest interview topic right now — be ready to explain what a hike does to borrowing, valuations, and bank earnings.

Wall Street staged a strong recovery on Monday, September 21. The S&P 500 rose 1.6% to 7,767 and the Nasdaq Composite jumped 2.3% to 27,139. The Dow was the exception, slipping 0.1% to 41,080.

That puts the S&P 500 back within reach of its all-time high of 7,816, set on June 1 — a quick turnaround after the index fell about 0.5% to around 7,650 following last week's Fed decision.

What's driving the rally

The biggest factor is oil. Brent crude fell for a fourth straight session and dropped below $100, which eases fears that energy costs will keep inflation high — and, by extension, that the Fed will have to hike aggressively. Optimism ahead of Wednesday's Trump-Xi summit is adding fuel.

Tech and chips lead the way

Technology and communication services stocks are leading. META is rallying ahead of its developers forum this week, and chipmakers Intel (INTC), AMD, and ARM are posting big gains. Tech was already the standout last week, finishing up 1.0% even as the broader market fell.

Who's lagging

Energy stocks are falling as oil retreats. Financials are also still under pressure after the sector dropped 2.3% last week, when Bank of America, Goldman Sachs, and Morgan Stanley warned that Q3 investment banking fees would come in below expectations.

Why this matters

One good day doesn't erase a tightening cycle. Watch whether the rally holds through the Trump-Xi summit and whether oil keeps falling. Notice the split, too: rate-sensitive tech is rallying on lower oil, while banks are weighed down by slow deal activity. Understanding why sectors move differently on the same news is exactly the kind of thinking interviewers look for.

Trump Hosts Xi at White House on Sept 24

The first Chinese state visit in over a decade comes with a trade truce about to expire and a possible $30 billion tariff deal on the table.

President Trump will host Chinese President Xi Jinping at the White House on Wednesday, September 24. It will be the first Chinese state visit to the US in more than a decade, and markets are watching closely.

The trade truce clock

The backdrop is the trade truce agreed in October 2025. Under that deal, the US paused certain tariffs in exchange for China continuing to export rare earth minerals — materials that are essential for chips, electronics, EVs, and defense equipment. That truce expires in roughly five weeks, so this summit is a key moment to extend or replace it.

What's being negotiated

A $30 billion tariff reduction framework covering goods on both sides is reportedly in the works. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has been leading talks with Chinese Vice Premier He Lifeng.

Why markets care

The outcome feeds directly into three things investors are already worried about: tech supply chains (especially anything relying on rare earths), inflation (tariffs raise import costs), and the performance of US and Chinese stocks. A deal could support the rally; a breakdown could reverse it quickly.

What to watch

Pay attention to three signals: whether the truce is extended, whether the $30 billion framework is announced, and how rare earth exports are handled. Then watch how chip stocks and the dollar react. Trade policy is a great example of how politics shows up in prices — a useful story to bring up in interviews.

Brent Crude Falls Below $100 for First Time in Weeks

Four straight days of falling prices, driven by diplomatic progress in the Iran conflict, are easing inflation pressure around the world.

By Aman Lathiya, Secretary  ·  Finance Club NYC  ·  September 21, 2026
$99.80
Brent crude per barrel
4 days
Consecutive declines
3.4%
US inflation it's feeding into
Hormuz
Key shipping route reopening

Brent crude, the global oil benchmark, has fallen for four consecutive sessions and slipped below $100 a barrel to $99.80 — the first time in weeks it's been under that level.

Why prices are falling

Two things are driving the drop: diplomatic progress in the Iran conflict, and improved tanker flows through the Strait of Hormuz. Hormuz is a narrow waterway between Iran and Oman that a large share of the world's seaborne oil passes through, so any disruption there sends prices up fast — and any easing brings them down.

The inflation connection

Energy prices have been one of the main reasons inflation has stayed stuck at 3.4%, forcing the Fed to raise rates last week. Cheaper oil lowers costs for transportation, shipping, and manufacturing, which can cool inflation across the whole economy. That's why the stock market rallied on Monday as oil fell.

Winners and losers

Energy stocks are falling along with oil prices. On the other side, consumers, airlines, and rate-sensitive sectors like tech benefit, since lower oil makes further aggressive Fed hikes less likely.

What this means for you

Lower oil usually shows up at the gas pump within a few weeks. For investors, it's a reminder that one commodity can move inflation, interest rates, and stock prices all at once. If diplomacy stalls and prices jump back above $100, expect the opposite reaction.

Stocks sold off after the Fed's September 16 hike, with the S&P 500 slipping about 0.5% to around 7,650. On Monday they bounced hard: the S&P 500 rose 1.6% to 7,767 and the Nasdaq gained 2.3% to 27,139. The Dow was the odd one out, down 0.1% at 41,080.

Three indexes, three different stories

The S&P 500 tracks 500 large US companies weighted by market value, so it's the best single read on the overall market. The Nasdaq Composite leans heavily toward tech, which is why it moves more on days when chips and software rally. The Dow holds just 30 stocks and is weighted by share price, not company size, so a few expensive stocks can move it a lot. That's how the Dow can fall on a day the other two rise.

Why tech led the bounce

Tech companies are valued on profits expected years from now. When inflation fears ease, investors expect fewer rate hikes, and those future profits are worth more today. Falling oil did exactly that on Monday. META rallied ahead of its developers forum, and chipmakers Intel, AMD, and ARM posted big gains.

Who got left behind

Energy stocks fell with oil. Financials are still under pressure after Bank of America, Goldman Sachs, and Morgan Stanley warned that Q3 investment banking fees would come in below expectations, dragging the sector down 2.3% last week.

What to watch next

The S&P 500 sits about 0.6% below its June 1 record of 7,816. Whether it breaks through depends on oil, the Trump-Xi summit outcome, and whether the Fed's next move stays at 25bps.

Try this yourself

Pick one day this week and check all three indexes plus the sector breakdown on a site like Yahoo Finance or the NYIT Bloomberg Terminal. Write one sentence on why they moved differently. Doing this weekly builds exactly the market sense interviewers test for.

Rates 101: The Fed Hike, Treasury Yields, and the Yield Curve

The Fed just started a new tightening cycle. Here's how the fed funds rate, the 10-year Treasury, and the yield curve fit together.

By Aman Lathiya, Secretary  ·  Finance Club NYC  ·  September 21, 2026
3.75–4.00%
Fed funds range
~4.98%
10-year Treasury
4.125%
Year-end median (dot plot)
88%
Odds of a Q4 hike

On September 16 the Fed raised its target range by 25 basis points to 3.75–4.00%, its first hike since July 2023. The 10-year Treasury yield touched 5% last week before easing to around 4.98% on Monday as oil fell.

Fed funds rate vs. the 10-year yield

The fed funds rate is the overnight rate banks charge each other, and the Fed sets it directly. The 10-year Treasury yield is set by the market. It reflects where investors think short rates, inflation, and growth are heading over the next decade. That's why the 10-year can fall on the same week the Fed hikes, if investors think the hikes will bring inflation down.

Why bond prices fall when yields rise

A bond pays a fixed coupon. If new bonds start paying more, your older, lower-paying bond is less attractive, so its price drops until its yield matches the market. Rising yields always mean falling prices for existing bonds. It's one of the most common interview questions out there.

The yield curve

The yield curve plots yields from short-term to long-term Treasuries. Normally longer bonds pay more. Right now the curve is slightly inverted, meaning some short-term yields sit above longer ones. Historically that's been read as a warning sign for slower growth, though it's not a perfect predictor.

What the dot plot tells us

The median projection of 4.125% for year-end implies one more 25bps hike. Markets price roughly an 88% chance of that happening in Q4. Inflation at 3.4% and core PCE at 3.7% are the reasons.

What this means for you

Variable-rate debt like credit cards gets more expensive right away. Savings accounts and CDs pay more. And if you’re recruiting, be ready to explain the price-yield relationship and what an inverted curve signals. Those two come up constantly.

Currencies: Why the Dollar Got Stronger and the Yen Didn’t

Higher US rates pulled money into the dollar last week, while Japan’s own rate hike surprised everyone by weakening the yen.

By Aman Lathiya, Secretary  ·  Finance Club NYC  ·  September 21, 2026
0.918
USD / EUR (−0.2%)
0.789
USD / GBP (flat)

DXY last week

The dollar gained 0.9% on the DXY index last week after the Fed hike. On Monday USD/EUR slipped 0.2% to 0.918, USD/GBP was flat at 0.789, and USD/JPY rose 0.3% to 150.25.

How to read a currency quote

USD/JPY at 150.25 means one US dollar buys 150.25 yen. If that number goes up, the dollar is strengthening against the yen. The DXY is an index measuring the dollar against a basket of major currencies, led by the euro, so it's the quickest way to see the dollar's overall direction.

Why rate hikes strengthen a currency

Money flows to where it earns more. When the Fed raises rates, holding dollars and dollar assets pays better, so global investors buy dollars. That demand pushes the dollar up.

The yen puzzle

The Bank of Japan raised its rate to 1.25%, its highest since 1995, yet the yen fell. The gap still matters more than the move: even after the hike, Japanese rates are far below US rates, so the incentive to hold dollars over yen barely changed. Markets had also largely expected the hike already.

The 155 line

Traders are watching USD/JPY near 155. Japan has stepped into currency markets before to slow a falling yen, and a move toward that level raises the odds of it happening again.

Why this matters

A strong dollar makes US exports pricier abroad and imports cheaper at home. It also hurts US companies that earn a lot overseas, since foreign sales convert into fewer dollars. Understanding that link between rates, currencies, and earnings is a big plus for any markets or corporate finance role.

Oil, Gold, and Bitcoin: Reading the Commodity Signals

Oil is the biggest macro story right now. Here's how it connects to inflation, and why gold and crypto moved the way they did.

By Aman Lathiya, Secretary  ·  Finance Club NYC  ·  September 21, 2026
$99.80
Brent crude / bbl
4 days
Of falling oil
$63,400
Bitcoin (+1.1%)
$2,450
Ethereum (+0.8%)

Brent crude fell for a fourth straight session to $99.80, dropping below $100 for the first time in weeks. Diplomatic progress in the Iran conflict and better tanker flows through the Strait of Hormuz are behind the decline.

Brent vs. WTI

Brent is the global benchmark, priced off North Sea crude. WTI is the US benchmark, priced at Cushing, Oklahoma. Brent reacts more to Middle East supply risk because so much seaborne oil moves through Hormuz, which helps explain why the gap between the two is wide right now.

Why oil drives inflation

Oil feeds into almost everything: gas, shipping, plane tickets, plastics, and manufacturing. When it stays high, inflation stays sticky, and the Fed keeps hiking. When it falls, the pressure eases. That chain is why stocks rallied on Monday as oil dropped.

Why gold fell after the hike

Gold pays no interest. When real rates (interest rates minus inflation) rise, holding cash or bonds becomes more rewarding than holding gold, so gold tends to slip. That's what happened after the Fed's move.

Crypto as a risk asset

Bitcoin rose 1.1% to $63,400 and Ethereum 0.8% to $2,450 on Monday, moving in step with tech stocks. Lately crypto has traded more like a high-risk growth asset than a hedge, rising when investors feel confident and falling when they don't.

What to watch

If diplomacy stalls and Brent jumps back above $100, expect inflation worries, and pressure on stocks, to return. Watch gas prices over the next few weeks too; they usually follow crude with a short lag.

What This Means for Finance Students
Rate Hike Impact on You
Higher fed funds rate = more expensive borrowing. Credit card interest rates go up. Student loan refinancing gets pricier. But savings account and CD yields also rise — if you have money sitting in a savings account, now is a good time to shop for a high-yield account. This is exactly what the Fed wants: slow down spending, reduce inflation.
Career Implications
Investment banking deal flow slows when rates are high — confirmed by weak Q3 fee guidance from Goldman Sachs and Bank of America. But fixed income desks, rates trading, and credit analysis roles become more active. If you're recruiting, highlight your understanding of interest rate environments. It's the hottest topic in every finance interview right now.
